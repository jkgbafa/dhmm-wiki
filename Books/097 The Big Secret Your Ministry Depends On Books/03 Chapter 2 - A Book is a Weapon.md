THE BURNING OF WYCLIFFE’S BOOKS

In 1382, William Courtenay obtained his dream to be inducted as the new Archbishop of Canterbury. His first and primary goal as the new archbishop was to deal with Wycliffe’s doctrines and followers. Courtenay called for a council to meet at Blackfriars to officially and formally condemn Wycliffe’s opinions. He invited nine other bishops and thirty-six graduates of theology to make a decision on twenty-four of Wycliffe’s writings¹.

In 1408, twenty-four years after Wycliffe’s death, Thomas Arundel, the Archbishop of Canterbury, summoned a group of clergy and decreed that no further translations of the Bible could be issued by way of book or tract and that no man was allowed to read such a translation, in private or public, as composed in the time of the said John Wycliffe... under penalty of the greater excommunication. If a person was caught with one of Wycliffe’s translations, he would lose his land and all his personal property to the Church².

In 1413, twenty-nine years after Wycliffe’s death, a papal decree ordered Wycliffe’s books to be burned³.

In 1415, thirty-one years after his death, the General Council of the Western church met in Constance and condemned Wycliffe’s teachings on three hundred accounts. They condemned his memory as “one who died an obstinate heretic” and ordered his bones to be exhumed from their resting place and “cast at a distance from the sepulchre.” At the time, a bishop by the name of Philip Repton was the head of the Lutterworth diocese. To his credit, Repton left Wycliffe’s grave untouched4.

In 1428, forty-four years after Wycliffe’s death, the pope (Pope Martin V) commanded that Wycliffe’s bones be exhumed and burned, and the new bishop of Lutterworth, Richard Fleming, carried out the task. After Wycliffe’s bones were exhumed and burned, the ashes were cast into the Swift River in an attempt to be free of any trace of him. But there was no chance of that. His memory was etched in the foundations of Christian liberty5.

THE BURNING OF WILLIAM TYNDALE’S BOOKS

William Tyndale translated the Bible into English, which was illegal at the time. At the time of Tyndale’s translation of the Bible into English (early 16th century), it was illegal to translate the Bible into vernacular languages (like English) without the approval of the Catholic Church or the monarchy. This was because the Church held the belief that only Latin (specifically the Vulgate) should be used for Scripture, as it was seen as the authorized and authoritative version of the Bible6. William Tyndale’s English New Testament (1526) was burned by church authorities in England7. He was later executed for heresy8.

THE BURNING OF MARTIN LUTHER’S BOOKS

Martin Luther’s writings challenged the Catholic Church, sparking the Protestant Reformation. His books, including The 95 Theses and On the Freedom of a Christian, were publicly burned by the Catholic Church.9 Martin Luther was excommunicated, but his ideas spread, leading to Protestantism.

THE BURNING OF JOHN CALVIN’S BOOKS

John Calvin’s writings were burned by both Catholic and some Protestant groups, especially in France.10 Despite this act of book burning, Calvinism became a major branch of Protestant Christianity.

THE BURNING OF JAN HUS’ BOOKS

Hus published a famous book called De Ecclesia (On the Church), which directly challenged papal authority and was a key reason for his execution.11 Hus preached reform and challenged the Catholic Church. His sermons and theological writings were destroyed. Hus was burned at the stake for heresy, but his followers (the Hussites) influenced later Protestant movements. Copies of Hus’ sermons, treatises, or letters were used to ignite the flames and were burned alongside him as a symbolic rejection. 12

THE BURNING OF WATCHMAN NEE’S BOOKS

Watchman Nee was a prominent Chinese Christian teacher and church leader. His works were considered dangerous by the Communist government. His Christian writings were banned and destroyed in China.13 Watchman Nee’s writings were burned as part of the political persecution of the day. He was arrested in 1952 and spent the rest of his life in prison until he died in 1972. Despite this, many of Watchman Nee’s books survived thanks to the efforts of his co-worker, Witness Lee, and the Living Stream Ministry.14 His books have been preserved, translated, and widely circulated.
